# MITI 4.2.1 global ratings reference (curated summary)

Source: Moyers, Manuel & Ernst (2015), MITI 4.2.1, section D and Appendix B. Paraphrased.

## How to assign a global score (D)

- One score from 1 to 5 per dimension for the whole interaction: a holistic, all-at-once
  judgment (gestalt), not an average of individual utterances.
- Start from a default of 3 and move up or down as the evidence indicates. A 3 may also reflect
  mixed practice.
- A 5 is generally not given when the segment contains prominent examples of poor practice.
- Judge the clinician relative to the designated change goal (section C).
- Behavior counts are NOT available to you; rate from the transcript alone.
- Cite turns that support AND turns that limit the score.

## Cultivating Change Talk (D)
How actively the clinician encourages the client's own language in favor of the change goal and
confidence for making it. Higher ratings require the change goal to be evident and the
conversation to be largely focused on change. Low when the clinician ignores change talk or
prioritizes history-taking, assessment or non-directive listening. Do not penalize the clinician
when the client does not offer change talk or does not respond to efforts to evoke it.
1. No explicit attention to or preference for the client's change language (e.g. only history of
   the problem, only education, supplies reasons for change instead of eliciting them, ignores
   change talk).
2. Sporadic attention to change language; frequently misses opportunities (superficial attention,
   no curiosity about values, strengths, past successes or benefits of change).
3. Often attends to change language but misses some opportunities (e.g. equal time to sustain and
   change talk once momentum for change has emerged).
4. Consistently attends to change language and makes efforts to encourage it (acknowledges and
   explores reasons for change; curious when change talk appears; may reflect it without always
   deepening it).
5. Marked and consistent effort to increase the depth, strength or momentum of change talk
   (shapes change language over a series of exchanges; strategically elicits and consistently
   responds; rarely misses opportunities).

## Softening Sustain Talk (D; Appendix B FAQ 6–8)
How much the clinician avoids focusing on reasons against change or for the status quo. Attending
to sustain talk to build rapport is acceptable, but only as long as needed. Low scores are
appropriate when the clinician gives considerable attention to barriers, even with MI-consistent
techniques. High scores are possible when no sustain talk occurs, as long as the clinician does
not evoke it (benefit of the doubt). Lower the score when sustain talk is rare but handled
inappropriately. In a true decisional balance that draws out both sides equally, the score is low
unless salvaged later. Consider the pattern and momentum of the whole session.
1. Consistently responds in ways that increase the frequency or depth of status-quo arguments
   (asks for reasons not to change, seeks elaboration of sustain talk, favors sustain talk over
   change talk when both occur).
2. Usually explores, focuses on or responds to reasons for the status quo (often deepens
   barriers; asks about barriers more than once; often reflects benefits of the status quo).
3. Gives preference to status-quo language but shows some instances of shifting focus away from
   it (some missed opportunities; attends to status-quo benefits even when change talk appears).
4. Typically avoids emphasizing status-quo language (does not ask for reasons not to change;
   minimal attention to sustain talk; does not linger on barriers).
5. Marked and consistent effort to decrease the depth, strength or momentum of status-quo
   language (e.g. structured tasks that redirect to the goal; double-sided reflections ending on
   change talk).

## Partnership (D; Appendix B FAQ 5)
How much the clinician conveys that expertise and wisdom about change reside mostly in the
client, behaving as two equal partners. Asking permission for nearly every comment, perfunctory
permission, not waiting for the answer, overstretching permission or "dumping" information may
lower Partnership even when PWP is coded.
1. Actively assumes the expert role for most of the interaction; collaboration absent (defines
   the problem, prescribes goals or plan, dominates, argues, frequent righting reflex).
2. Superficially responds to opportunities to collaborate (rarely gives up the expert role;
   minimal querying of client input; supplies expertise instead of joint problem solving;
   occasionally corrects the client).
3. Incorporates client contributions in a lukewarm or erratic way (takes some opportunities but
   does not structure for them; righting reflex largely absent; a stand-off).
4. Fosters collaboration so the client's contributions shape the session (seeks agreement on
   problem, agenda and goals; engages client in problem solving; does not push when the client
   pushes back).
5. Actively fosters power sharing so the client substantially influences the session (negotiates
   agenda and goals; client evaluates options and plans; explicitly treats client as expert and
   decision maker; tailors advice to client input; favors strengths over deficits).

## Empathy (D)
How much the clinician understands, or tries to grasp, the client's perspective and conveys it
("trying on" what the client thinks and feels). Not the same as warmth, sympathy or advocacy.
1. Little or no attention to the client's perspective (only information-seeking questions).
2. Sporadic efforts; understanding may be inaccurate or detract from the client's meaning.
3. Actively trying to understand, with modest success (a few accurate reflections, may miss the
   point).
4. Active, repeated efforts; accurate understanding, mostly limited to explicit content.
5. Deep understanding of the client's view, including what the client means but has not said
   (many accurate complex reflections; encourages elaboration beyond what is needed to follow the
   story).
